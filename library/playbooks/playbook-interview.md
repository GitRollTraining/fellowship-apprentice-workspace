# Interview playbook — eliciting an undocumented process from the person who runs it

An executable instruction set for the fellow. Each numbered step names, in brackets, the assessed
competency it teaches; the set of competencies taught here and the set assessed are the same set, and the
names are carried inline so the two cannot drift apart silently. Two parts carry no name — step 3, the
opening, and the unnumbered capture section — because both are craft the assessment does not separately
judge, and they are marked by their absence rather than given a label that does not exist.

## The assumption this rests on, stated on its face

This playbook is written to be executed by an agent, and the same content is what a fellow is taught.
**The assumption is that an agent handed this playbook in context is a proxy for a person who studied
it.** That is cheap and defensible — the know-how is propositional, and an agent with it in context
against an agent without it is the closest available comparison. It is still an assumption. It is not
established that an agent's execution predicts a person's, and nothing here should be read as evidence
that it does.

**What is not established: that any of this works.** The closest published test of teaching an interview
method to novices — the evaluation Militello and colleagues ran on their own Applied Cognitive Task
Analysis method in 1997, matched, randomly assigned, blind-coded, on people screened for no domain
knowledge — found **no statistically significant difference** in the quality of what the taught and
untaught groups came back with — there were differences, none of them significant — and a significant
difference only in how confident they felt. That study is underpowered (n about twelve), so it is not
evidence of no effect either. **Nobody has shown that following this playbook collects more or better
than not following it.** One instruction inside it does have a controlled result behind it, and it is
step 14.

**This playbook is written blind.** It was authored from the assessed competencies and the sources alone.
It names no case, no persona, no scenario and no scoring mechanism, and it was not tuned against anything
that might later measure it. A playbook written against the thing that scores it measures the authoring
rather than the know-how.

**It has never been executed.** No fellow has run it against a real business owner, and no result of any
kind has come back from it.

## What you are doing

One person runs a business. Part of how they run it has never been written down — they just do it. Your
job is to leave the conversation able to write that process down precisely enough that someone else, or
something else, can run it, and precisely enough that the business owner can check the result without
you.

**You are alone.** There is no second person taking notes, no observer, no colleague to rehearse against,
and nobody recruiting participants for you. Every instruction below is executable by one person.

## Preconditions

Do not start the session until all four hold.

1. You can name the one decision this session has to settle.
2. You have the owner's agreement to record, and you know what you may do with the recording afterwards. **Without it, say so before you start rather than after.** You may still run the session from your own notes, but be clear about what that costs: step 19 becomes impossible — you cannot audit questioning you have no record of, and memory returns the session you meant to run — step 17's ambiguity list has to be built live rather than reconstructed, and **step 16 degrades to self-reference**, because your pointers then resolve to your own notes rather than to anything a reader could check without you, which is the whole point of that step. Treat a no-recording session as a first pass to be repeated, not as the record.
3. The pre-session note from step 1 is written down, not held in your head.
4. You have read your own question list once and repaired it (step 2).

---

## Before

### 1. Decide what the session must settle  (competency: writing the pre-session note, and closing it afterwards)

Write, before anything else:

- **The decision.** One sentence: what will be decided or built differently because this conversation happened. If you cannot write it, do not write a question list yet.
- **Three lists.** What you already know. What you are assuming. What you do not know. Every item goes in exactly one list. The assumption list is the one that matters — it is where you will be wrong.
- **What is out of scope for this session**, so you can decline a tangent without deciding on the spot.

Keep this note. You come back to it at step 18.

### 2. Write the questions, then repair them  (competency: repairing your own drafted question list, keeping both versions)

Draft your questions, then go through your own draft and rewrite every question that fails any of these
three tests. **Keep both versions.** The repair is the work.

| Test | It fails if | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| One thing | It joins two questions with "and", or asks about two things at once | Split it into two questions |
| No answer inside | It suggests what the answer should be — "Do you find that slow?", "That must be frustrating?" | Ask the open form — "What happens next?", "What is that like?" |
| No evaluation inside | It carries a judgment about the work — "Why do you still do that manually?" | Remove the judgment — "Walk me through how that gets done" |

A question with the answer inside it returns your own guess in the owner's voice. That is the failure
that matters here: the process you write down would be the one you assumed, not the one the business
runs.

Order the questions broad to narrow, and put nothing important last.

## During

### 3. Open

Say who you are, what the conversation is for, and that you are not testing them. Confirm the recording
agreement out loud, on the record. Start with something easy to answer about their role or their day, so
the conversation has a rhythm before you need anything from it.

### Capturing while you are the one asking

You are moderating and recording at the same time, and the published guidance mostly assumes you are not.
Three procedures written for exactly this case, and one place where they disagree:

- **Best option: fill a labelled table the owner can see as you fill it.** Set up the headings first and write their answers in as they talk, on whatever surface both of you can see: a shared document, a screen, a whiteboard, a sheet of paper. **Use the columns step 7 actually asks for — step · who does it · tools and records touched · where it waits · what goes wrong** — not the ones the source method uses. The published version of this technique, Applied Cognitive Task Analysis, was built to surface what makes a task cognitively hard, and its columns are "why it is hard" and "cues and strategies". We are after process flow, so the technique transfers and its schema does not. This does two jobs at once: the table is the record, so you need no note-taker, and the owner watches it being filled and corrects it live, so the CONTENT gets checked as you go. It does not replace the reviewer — nobody is critiquing how you are asking, which is what step 19 is for. It is the published solution to the capture problem and it costs nothing.
- **Do not stop the flow to get something right.** When a name, a number or a term goes past that you did not catch, write it phonetically with a one-word clue to where in the story it sat, and ask for the correct form *after* the session. Interrupting to spell a word costs you the thread you were pulling.
- **Write keywords and a timestamp, not sentences.** Decide the handful of things you are listening for before you start; when one appears, note the word and the time. The recording holds the rest.
- **Published practice disagrees on when to write up, and you are choosing rather than inheriting.** One procedure says write up within 10-20 minutes while it is fresh; another says wait a day, reconstruct from the question list first, and only then fill gaps from the recording. **This playbook takes the write-it-while-fresh side** — step 15 says the same day, and sooner is better — because a solo practitioner with no second account of the session has nothing to correct a decayed memory against.

### 4. Ask one thing at a time  (competency: asking one thing at a time, with no expected answer, evaluation or proposed solution inside the question)

Hold to the three tests from step 2 live, not only on the draft. When you notice mid-sentence that you
have loaded a question, stop and re-ask it. That is not an error to hide; it is the behaviour.

Stay quiet after asking. The pause is doing work. Do not fill it.

### 5. Get a real instance, not a general account  (competency: getting one datable recent instance and recording what actually happened in it)

Ask for the most recent actual time this happened. Get:

- **When** it was — a date or a nameable occasion.
- **What set it off** — the trigger, not the schedule.
- **What was actually done**, in order.
- **What it cost** — time, money, someone else's attention.
- **How it ended** — including whether it ended well.

### 6. Pull generalities back to the last real time  (competency: pulling a generality back to the most recent actual occasion, and marking which statements rest on an instance)

When you hear "we usually", "normally", "typically", "I'd probably", ask: **"When was the last time that
happened? Take me through that one."**

Then mark, in your record, which statements rest on a real instance and which are still generalisations.
Do not silently promote a generalisation into a requirement. Both may survive; only one of them is
evidence.

### 7. Reconstruct the process end to end  (competency: reconstructing one process from trigger to finished output — steps in order, who does each, tools and records touched, where the work waits)

Ask them to walk you through it as it actually runs, from trigger to finished result. Better, ask them to
show you. Capture:

- the steps, in the order they happen
- who does each one
- which tools, files and records get touched
- where the work stops and waits on somebody else

Watch for the parts they skip. People start stories in the middle and leave out what they do without
thinking. When the account jumps, ask what happened in between.

### 8. Get the exceptions  (competency: recording what happens when the normal path does not apply, as named cases rather than a category)

Ask what happens when the normal path does not apply, and get the cases, not the category. "Sometimes
there are exceptions" is not an answer. **"Tell me about the last time it did not go that way"** is the
question.

### 9. Ask the next question from the last answer  (competency: asking the next question from something the owner has just said, so each follow-up points back at the answer that prompted it)

Your list is a floor, not a script. After each answer the next question should come from what they just
said — the surprising detail, the unexplained word, the step that implies another step.

Use their own words back: *"You said <their phrase> — what happened there?"*

If you reach the end of your list without ever departing from it, the session failed regardless of how
much you collected: a prepared list cannot anticipate a process nobody has written down.

### 10. Chase contradictions and surprises  (competency: following a contradiction or a surprise rather than returning to the planned topic)

When something contradicts what they said earlier, or contradicts what you assumed, **stop and follow
it**. Do not note it politely and return to your topic. A contradiction is usually the highest-value
moment in the session — it commonly means there are two paths through the process and only one has been
described.

Ask without accusation: *"Earlier you mentioned <A>, and just now <B> — help me understand how those fit
together."*

**When you get a black-box answer.** The owner will sometimes say, in effect, "I don't know how I do
that, I just do." This is the normal response of somebody who is genuinely expert at something they have
never had to explain, and it is not a refusal. A published probe set exists for it — the fixed-probe
structured interview described by Shadbolt and Smart (2015) — and these are the questions to reach for:

> *"Could you tell me about a typical case?"* · *"Why would you do that?"* · *"How would you do that?"* ·
> *"When would you do that — is that always the case?"* · *"What alternatives are there?"* · *"What if
> <the thing that is normally true> were not true?"* · *"Tell me about an unusual case."*

Those are seven of the nine published probes, in order. Two more exist and are left out here on purpose:
one asks for the last case encountered, which step 5 already does, and one is a bare "tell me more", to
be used only if the owner dries up.

The second and third turn a statement into a rule. The fourth and sixth find its edges. Beware the opposite
failure too, which is documented in the same source: an expert can give you a perfectly good decision
and then a spurious explanation of why they made it. What they DID is evidence; why they say they did it
is a claim.

### 11. Say it back and get it corrected  (competency: saying the key statement back in your own words and recording whether the owner confirmed or corrected it, before it is written into anything)

Before any important statement is written down as fact, say it back in your own words and ask them to
correct it. Not *"does that make sense?"* — that gets a yes. Say the substance:

> *"So the way I understand it: when <trigger> happens you <action>, and if <condition> you instead
> <alternative>. Where is that wrong?"*

**Ask where it is wrong, not whether it is right.** Record what they confirmed and, more importantly,
what they corrected. A correction is a better outcome than a confirmation, and the record should show
which one happened.

### 12. Ask the boundary questions  (competency: asking and recording what must not be automated, who has to approve before the work continues, and which information may not leave the business)

These do not come up on their own, and the session is not finished without them:

- **What must never be done automatically?** What always needs a person to look at it.
- **Who has to approve, and before what?**
- **What information must not leave the business?**

Get the answers, not the acknowledgement that you asked.

### 13. Ask what happens when it breaks  (competency: asking and recording what the owner does today when an input is missing or wrong, and what happens downstream when the process fails)

- What do you do when the information you need is missing or wrong?
- What happens downstream when this goes wrong — who finds out, and how?
- What has actually gone wrong before?

### 14. Close — and use this exact wording  (competency: closing with a coverage question and recording what it surfaced)

Ask what you did not ask about, in this form:

> ***"Is there something else you want to address today?"***

**Not "anything else."** This is the single best-measured instruction in the whole playbook and the
difference is one word. The studied wording was *"Is there something else you want to address in the
visit today?"* — "in the visit" is dropped here because a business conversation is not a visit. **The
load-bearing word is *something*, and that is what must not change.** In a controlled comparison across
20 physicians and 224 patients, the SOME form eliminated 78% of the patients' unaddressed concerns (odds
ratio 0.154, p = .001); the ANY form could not be distinguished from asking nothing at all (p = .122).
Neither form made the conversation longer.

What is graded is that the coverage question was asked and its answer recorded, not the syllable. The
measured wording is the taught default and the reason the step exists.

Then say what happens next with what they told you. Promise nothing that is not yet decided.

## After

Do steps 15 and 16 the same day, before anything else. What you remember degrades fast, and it degrades
in the direction of what you expected to hear.

### 15. Write the record with heard and concluded kept apart  (competency: writing the discovery record with what was heard kept separate from what was concluded from it)

Write what you heard first, before interpreting any of it. One statement per entry, in their words. Then,
separately and visibly marked, what you conclude from it.

A reader must be able to tell which is which without asking you. If you cannot separate them in the
writing, you have not separated them in your head.

### 16. Point every load-bearing statement back at the session  (competency: pointing every load-bearing statement back at the place in the session it came from)

Every statement the process rests on gets a pointer to where in the session it came from — the turn, the
timestamp, the document you were shown. Someone must be able to check the record without you in the room.

A pointer that does not resolve is worse than no pointer. It is a fail, not a blemish.

### 17. List what is still ambiguous  (competency: listing the ambiguous terms, unexplained numbers and contradictions, each with a disposition)

List every vague term, unexplained number and contradiction from the session, and give each a
disposition: **settled in the session**, or **still open**.

An empty list after a real conversation means you did not look, and counts as a fail rather than a clean
run. Ambiguity is the normal state of an undocumented process; the list is the honest output, and the
still-open items are the agenda for next time.

Where a still-open item is a decision the owner could not make in the room, run `/to-questionnaire`
on it. It turns the question into something they, or whoever actually owns the answer, can fill in
between sessions, so an unanswered question leaves as a document rather than as a gap you carry.
The skill only runs when you type it; nothing will offer it to you.

### 18. Close the loop on the pre-session note  (competency: marking after the session which unknowns closed and which did not)

Return to step 1. Mark each unknown closed or still unknown, and each assumption confirmed, corrected or
untested. This is what says whether the session did its job.

### 19. Audit your own questioning  (competency: auditing your own recorded session against the same three tests, and naming what you will do differently next time)

Listen back to your own recording and mark your own questions against the three tests from step 2. Then
write one line: **what you will do differently in the next session.**

Nobody else is going to review your interviewing. The published substitute for the colleague who would
have critiqued you is you, against your own recording, to a fixed checklist — not from memory, which
returns the session you meant to run.

Ask yourself, in this order: Were the questions open? Did any of them carry the answer? How many
follow-ups came from an answer rather than from the list? Did I sit through the silences or fill them?
Did the owner actually answer what I asked?

**The honest limit on this step:** practitioners are documented to be poor judges of their own
interviewing, and in one published finding those relying on experience alone scored below people fresh
out of training. This is a real procedure and a weaker one than external review. Treat a clean self-audit
as the weakest of the checks here, not the strongest.

## Handing it back

### 20. Write the owner's version  (competency: writing the owner's account of the finished process in the owner's own vocabulary)

Write the account **for the owner**: what the process does, what it will not do, and what to check.

Use the words they used. If a term of art did not come out of their mouth during the session, either
leave it out or define it in their words on the spot — ordinary English is not the constraint, unexplained
jargon is. This is not the specification and it is not a summary of your record — it is the thing
they will read when you are not there.

### 21. Find out what they cannot answer from it, and fix that  (competency: recording what the owner could not answer from the handover document unaided, and revising until they can)

Give it to them and ask them to tell you what the process does, and what to do when it fails, **from the
document, without you helping**. Record what they could not answer. Revise. Repeat.

You are finished when the owner can act on the document without you — check the result, spot when it has
gone wrong, and say what the process is. **That is the standard the programme sets:** the capability is
exercised on bounded, repeatable work that someone in the business can verify without the fellow's help.
Whether the owner can also re-execute the whole process by hand is a higher bar that the programme does
not set, because the agent is what executes; treat it as a bonus, not the exit condition.

A published procedure-writing standard sets a HIGHER bar than the programme does, worth holding yourself
to as an aspiration rather than as the exit condition. The US Environmental Protection Agency's guidance
on preparing standard operating procedures requires that someone with limited experience of the procedure
should be able to reproduce it unsupervised, and that the draft be tested by somebody other than the
person who wrote it. That is reproduce, not verify — a different and larger standard than the one this
programme is held to, and it must not be cited as if it were the same one.

## Stop conditions

Stop and escalate rather than continue:

- **You do not have consent to record, and the process is one nobody could reconstruct from notes alone.** Say so before the session, not after.
- **You are asked to write down something you have been told is confidential, in a form that would leave the business.** That is a boundary from step 12 and it outranks the record.
- **The process depends on a judgment nobody in the business can check, including the owner.** Work no one in the room can verify is outside what this engagement covers, and continuing produces something that looks finished and is not.
- **The owner is not the person who does the work.** You are being told how it is supposed to run. Get to the person who runs it, or record that you did not.

## Failure modes

The ones that look like success from the inside.

| It looks like | It actually is | The check |
|---|---|---|
| A long, cooperative, pleasant session | You asked loaded questions and got agreement | Read your own record for questions with the answer inside — step 2's three tests, applied afterwards |
| A complete process, start to finish | A general account with nothing datable behind it | Count how many steps trace to a real instance (step 6) |
| Full coverage of your question list | You never left the list, so you learned only what you already knew to ask | Count how many follow-ups came from an answer rather than from the list (step 9) |
| Confident notes, written up cleanly | Your inferences are in there wearing the owner's voice | Step 15's separation, checked by a reader who was not present |
| The owner agreed with everything | You asked *"does that make sense?"* instead of *"where is that wrong?"* | Step 11, phrased as an invitation to correct |
| No ambiguities to report | You did not look | Step 17 — an empty list is a finding about the interviewer |

These are diagnostics for the practitioner, not a scoring key. Nothing here rates a person: the programme
grades pass or fail against a floor, never a rank.

## Not in the playbook

**All nineteen assessed competencies appear above**, because this playbook and the list of assessed
competencies are two renderings of one thing.

What is deliberately absent is the material ruled out when the scope of this work was set, listed so an
omission is a decision on the record rather than a gap.

| Absent | Why |
|---|---|
| Screening and recruiting participants | There is one owner, and the pairing is decided before the fellow arrives. Ruled out |
| Assigning a note-taker or observer; rehearsing the guide with a teammate; team debrief and group affinity clustering | Nobody to assign, rehearse with or debrief with. Ruled out. The published sources assume all four; the competencies those roles carried are re-homed onto the one practitioner in steps 2, 15 and 17 |
| Presenting or defending the work to an audience under questioning | The programme's own four-item statement of what a graduate can do — elicit, specify, direct and build, verify — contains no such requirement. Deferred rather than ruled out, because the requirement is silent rather than adverse |
| Communicating with a teammate about the work | Same. Deferred |
| Pricing, commercial scoping, selling the engagement | The programme explicitly does not teach client acquisition, pricing or commercial scoping of the engagement. Ruled out |
| The consent procedure itself — what to say, retention, withdrawal | Named as a gate in the preconditions but not specified here. The programme does not yet own it: you cannot honestly teach a fellow to tell an owner what happens to the recording before the programme has decided what happens to the recording. Deferred, and carried as an open item |
| Any scoring, rating, ranking or rubric | Evidence is graded as a pass/fail floor check, never a ranked score, and no assessment instrument is built here |

**What these do not transfer, stated with them.** Oral history, journalism and clinical intake invert our
expertise relation — there the interviewer is the expert and the informant describes their own
experience, whereas here the owner is the expert and you are not. Those supply a HOW without a WHAT.
The knowledge-elicitation sources (Applied Cognitive Task Analysis and the fixed probe set) do NOT invert
it: a non-specialist interviewing a domain expert is exactly this job, which is why they carry the most
weight above. Across all of them the measured effects were obtained under a known completeness criterion
— a previsit survey, a scored ground truth — and a business process has none, so the effect sizes are a
reason to use the techniques and not a prediction of what they will produce here.

**Step 9 carries no citation on purpose.** The claim that a follow-up's value is the new information
it produces rather than the number asked is **this curriculum's own design principle, not an empirical
finding.** Both papers cited for it in the source material were read and neither states it; the closer of
the two names "the right balance between asking too many and too few follow-up questions" as the open
problem. Labelling it house method is the honest form, and the gap is carried as an open item.
